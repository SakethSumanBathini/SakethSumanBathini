A leak shows up. Nobody can say where it came from.

Problem statement (59 words)

When a university exam paper leaks on WhatsApp, nobody can tell which of its 100-plus colleges leaked it. Leakers post a few typed or cropped questions without QR codes or printed IDs, so sources surface only by luck or police work, and every student risks a re-exam. It happened at Kannur University and Karnataka State Law University in 2025.

Students

Lose an exam they prepared for and sit a re-exam, punished for one college's leak.

Universities

Pay for re-exams and observers in every college, and lose credibility each time.

Investigators

Start with no lead and spend days or weeks on phone forensics.

Every current fix breaks when someone retypes five questions.

Giving each recipient a slightly different copy is an old idea: spies call it a canary trap, and IBM filed a patent on a version for exam papers in 2003 (granted 2007). We found no deployed version that survives retyping, rewording and partial leaks across hundreds of colleges and states its own error rate.

Same questions, same marks. A different pattern for every college.

Each college's paper puts the questions in its own order and uses its own approved wording, like “Compare a stack and a queue” or “How does a stack differ from a queue?”. Marks, content and difficulty stay the same. That pattern is the college's code, and it survives retyping.

Read the small grids like an OMR sheet. Columns A to J are the ten questions every college gets, rows are where each one is printed, and the number in a filled bubble is the wording that college got. Point at or tap a question to compare.

      Even an answer key gives the college away.

        Numbers work the same way

        For a numerical question, the paper-setter approves several sets of values chosen to be of equal difficulty, and each college gets one. Reworded or not, a leaked question has to keep its numbers, or its answer changes.

        Then we tried to fool it.

        148,000 simulated leaks against the decoder in the demo above, 2,000 for each setting, almost all with typing mistakes. Tricks can stop it naming anyone.         “Names the source from” is the smallest leak named in at least 95% of cases. The rewording and shuffling rows are what the answer-key and numbers layer is for. Before we hardened the decoder on 18 Oct, the same 148,000 leaks named 8 innocent colleges. Three changes, plus a bug fix in the second check: numbers that simply run 1, 2, 3 may be the leaker’s own, so both readings are weighed; a line’s wording counts only when it clearly matches one approved wording; and the bar went from 1,000 to 10,000 to 1.

        A bound on false names. Proven, then tested.

        The naming bar is not a hunch. Take a leak that came from none of the 500 colleges, made up or copied from elsewhere. Under our model of such copies, the chance that it clears the bar for naming a college is at most 1 in 10,000 (2 in 10,000 counting the separate check for two-college mixes), and that stays true however many times investigators re-run the trace as more lines turn up. The proof is short: a likelihood ratio averages to one on such copies, so it rarely grows large (Markov's and Ville's inequalities; see the paper).

        We then checked it on 800,000 simulated outside copies, read one line at a time. At the 10,000-to-1 bar, 11 of 200,000 realistic short-question copies and 14 of 400,000 answer keys ever crossed it, inside the bound; in the attack test the full decoder named 1 of 32,000 outside copies. Wrong names for real leaks are measured, not bounded: in our tests no real leak was pinned on the wrong college.

        Only one step of today's delivery changes.

        Universities already send each college a PDF. Leak Trace sits at the moment those PDFs are made, and again when a leak appears.

        Before the exam

        1. 1Setter writes one paper

          And approves wordings, number sets and which questions may move.

        2. 2Two of three officials unlock

          The secret key is split three ways; any two make the 500 versions. No table is stored.

        3. 3Per-college PDFs, keys and a seal

          A fingerprint of all 500 PDFs, signed by two officials, goes to every principal first.

        4. 4Existing portal delivers

          OTP or PIN as today; each college prints its own copy.

        After a leak

        1. 5Leak spotted

          A phone photo, a few retyped questions, or an answer key on WhatsApp.

        2. 6Read it

          OCR reads photos; fuzzy matching ties typo-ridden lines to the master questions.

        3. 7Compare with all 500

          A likelihood for every college; a second stage checks for mixed copies.

        4. 8Ranked lead

          Names a college only when it is 10,000 times likelier than any other, and 10,000 × 500 times likelier than an outside copy; otherwise a shortlist.

        How it answers the six judging criteria.

        Problem clarity
        Real, recent leaks at Kannur, KSLU, PJTSAU and the Karnataka SSLC exams. In none of them did the leaked paper itself say where it came from.
        Originality
        Builds openly on the canary trap and IBM's 2003 patent. New: three layers of content-identical marks, including answer keys and numbers that survive rewording, and a proven bound on naming a college for a copy from outside the system.
        Demonstrated feasibility
        A working prototype: this live demo, OCR on phone photos, 135,000 whole-paper and 148,000 attack leaks, answer-key and numbers tests, and key custody with 12 of 12 checks passing.
        Cost
        Software only, all open source. It changes one step, making each college's PDF, and tracing runs on a laptop in under a second.
        Beneficiary impact
        A re-exam at one college instead of all. Kannur's sub-committee recommended exactly that once the source was known.
        Scalability
        The same method serves state boards and paper-delivery vendors that reach 2,000+ colleges; more colleges need about one more leaked line, because the bar rises with their number.

        Different order, same exam.

        • We reorder only where we expect order not to change difficulty: equal-mark short questions, either/or blocks, MCQ options and sub-parts.
        • Related questions stay together and the easy-to-hard order stays fixed.
        • A faculty member approves every wording and every set of numbers, answer keys are generated for each version, and value sets are calibrated in a pilot before high-stakes use.
        • Every college's answer key uses each letter 3 to 7 times out of 20, with 8 to 12 right answers in the middle positions B and C, so no college's key is lopsided.
        • Average marks for each version are published after the exam.

        What it cannot do yet.

        • All results so far are simulated. Real leaks made by real people are the next test.
        • English only for now. OCR for Telugu, Kannada, Tamil and Malayalam is untested.
        • A leaker who rewords and shuffles every short question erases the first two layers; then only numbers and answers can name the college. In our tests it gave a shortlist rather than blame an innocent college. A trace is a lead to confirm, not proof.
        • An answer key needs about 14 correct answers to name a college; a key full of mistakes gives a shortlist.
        • Leaks from the setter or the press happen before versions exist. A leaked answer key that matches the master copy tells investigators to look upstream; for question text, Leak Trace holds back.
